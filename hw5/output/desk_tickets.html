Reflection

My read on the full run. Evidence comes from the three ticket tabs, the Cash tab, and the run data in audit_trail.json.

The short version: the team got every ticket right. All three were resolved, no agent touched money, and checking ended at exactly $152.00. But it got there expensively: 6 team runs, 80 model calls, and about 254k tokens for one $28 tee, one rent check, and one discount quote. The run also showed something no single ticket was about: the shop is now nearly out of cash.

#101, Bulldog tee for Tauhid Zaman: correct, but the costliest ticket by far.

  • The facts were right. 0 size-S tees on hand. Bulldog Print Co was blocked by invoice 501 ($840, 3 days overdue) with a 5-day lead time.
  • The order of steps was the only one that works: pay invoice 501 (request #1), then buy the one tee ($8, request #3), then tell Tauhid it arrives 2026-09-05. Until the order was paid, no draft promised a date the shop couldn't keep.
  • The cost was high: 135k tokens for a $28 sale, about 53% of the whole run. Two causes:
    • Duplicate work. In run 2, Inventory was started twice, once by the Boss and once by Accounting, so it repeated list_vendors, get_invoice_status, check_stock_and_margin, get_ticket and get_desk.
    • Draft clutter. Customer Service left three drafts for Tauhid (#1, #4 "Updated update on your Classic Bulldog Tee…", and #6). Only #6 is current, so a person reviewing drafts has to work out which one to use.

#102, Rent from Elm City Properties: correct and quick, plus two avoidable extras.

  • What went right: Facilities did its job. It checked the landlord's email against lease 1 ($2,400, due 2026-09-02, not overdue), Accounting filed request #2, and once I approved it the rent was paid and next_due moved to 2026-10-02.
  • Extra 1, a Customer Service detour. The Boss had Customer Service write two notes to the landlord (#2 "Re: Rent due" and #5 "…payment confirmed"). Nothing asked for that, and Customer Service's role is customers.
  • Extra 2, a pointless resume. My approval arrived while run 1 was still working, so the ticket was already resolved. The approve route still resumed it anyway: 22,256 tokens to confirm something already done. That's a design flaw in the app, not the agents' fault.

#103, Bulk hoodies for Yale AI Club: the best judgment of the three.

  • The call was sound. 10% off ($52.20 a hoodie) leaves a $30.20 margin over the $22 cost. It declined the 12-unit restock because 12 × $22 = $264 and only $160 was left after #101 and #102 were paid. No purchase order, no payment, and draft #3 makes no restock promise.
  • The stated reason was imprecise. The Boss said "rent takes priority". That came from Accounting pointing out the October rent ($2,400, due 10-02). But $160 can't cover that rent either, so holding the cash doesn't help. The real reason was simply $264 > $160.
  • The offer was conservative. It offered only the 8 in stock, with no "the other 12 once cash allows". That probably loses most of a 20-hoodie order.

2Actual vs. Expected, ticket by ticket

The plan predicted the main path well. It failed to predict the optional peer-to-peer asks, and it was overtaken by a definition change I made before the run.

  • #101 matched on: the first call (Inventory), Boss → Inventory / Accounting / Customer Service, the $840 request, and the $8 purchase order after approval.
    It differed on: an unplanned Accounting → Inventory ask, the Boss also checking list_open_tickets, and a final status of resolved instead of waiting_on_vendor. Before the run I redefined "resolved" to include "order paid, customer told the arrival date".
  • #102 matched on: the first call (Facilities), Boss → Accounting, the $2,400 request, and the payment effects (−$2,400; next due 10-02).
    It differed on: Customer Service was brought in although the plan said it wouldn't be, the possible Facilities → Accounting ask didn't happen, and the ticket was resolved in the first run because my approval landed mid-run.
  • #103 matched on: the first call (Inventory, in the same step as Accounting), a discount inside the expected 10–20% range, the restock deferred for cash, and no payment.
    It differed on: the predicted Accounting → Inventory ask didn't happen, the Boss skipped get_cash_position and relied on Accounting, the offer covered only 8 hoodies, and the final status was resolved instead of in_progress.

Takeaway: who the Boss calls first is predictable, because it follows the ticket type and the links on the ticket. Whether a specialist asks another specialist isn't: the peer ask the plan expected on #102 and #103 didn't happen, and an unexpected one did on #101.

3What would have been simpler as one agent with tools

Honestly, all three tickets. The shop has one database and the relevant tools number about a dozen, and each ticket needs 4–8 of them. Splitting the work across five agents mostly added overhead:

  • Repeated reads: almost every agent called get_ticket and get_desk again, and Accounting called get_cash_position on every ticket.
  • Repeated context: every delegation had to restate the ticket in the question ("For ticket #103: rent_notice… lease 1…").
  • Repeated agents: on #101, Inventory ran twice in one run.

That's why the run took about 254k tokens.

  • #102 is the clearest single-agent case. get_ticket → get_lease_and_cash → request_payment_approval → update_ticket is four calls. It used 24 model calls across two runs.
  • #101 is really a fixed state machine: invoice 501 paid → file the tee purchase order → after it's paid, draft the arrival date. Plain workflow code, or one agent with a checklist, would handle it more reliably than an LLM rediscovering the next step on each resume, and it would never produce three drafts.
  • #103 is a single judgment call (pick a discount, decide whether to restock). One agent with check_stock_and_margin, quote_discount and get_cash_position would make it with less back-and-forth.

What the team actually bought us is permissions and voice, not capability.

  • Customer Service can't file payments, and Accounting can't write to customers.
  • The customer-facing prompt keeps unit costs, vendor names and invoice 501 out of Tauhid's and Yale AI Club's drafts.
  • But the most important rule, that a human approves every payment, is enforced in code (the approval key and the approve route), not by the team split.

The split would pay off at higher volume (tickets in parallel), with much longer role prompts, or with a different model per role. Locked to gpt-6-luna for every agent, we didn't get that last benefit.

4Three new problems this team could solve with the tools we built

Each assumes the ticket is already on the board. Every number is from the working database after the run (checking $152.00).

A. A Crest Mug order: the #101 pattern with an unblocked vendor

A customer wants a dozen Crest Mugs. check_stock_and_margin shows 0 on hand (cost $3.50, list $14). list_vendors finds Elm City Gifts (mugs and small goods, 3-day lead, no open invoice, so not blocked). Accounting files a purchase order for 12 × $3.50 = $42, which fits in $152. I approve it, cash drops to $110, it arrives 2026-09-03, and Customer Service drafts that date. Every tool already exists, and unlike #101 there's no invoice to clear first.

B. Tauhid switches to a size M

If Tauhid writes back "an M is fine and I need it this week", check_stock_and_margin shows 5 size-M tees on hand. Customer Service drafts "ready for pickup now" and the Boss resolves it with no money. Customer Service already had the stock tool to see this; it just wasn't asked. The $8 S-size order already placed stays in transit for the next customer.

C. A bulk order that stock can fully cover

Another student group asks for 30 Yale Caps at a discount. check_stock_and_margin shows 40 on hand (cost $6, list $22). quote_discount at 15% gives $18.70 each, $561.00 total, $12.70 margin per cap, well above cost. It's #103 without the cash squeeze: no purchase order, no payment, just the Boss's call and a draft. The same works for hoodies in size L (14 on hand).

5Three new problems this team could not solve, and what it would need

A. Making the sale and handling stock: money in, goods out, deliveries received

Yale AI Club's $417.60 is never recorded. Inventory still shows 8 size-M hoodies as if none were sold, and 0 size-S tees, with no way to add the one arriving 2026-09-05. Our tools only read inventory, and cash only goes out.

Would need: an orders table and MCP tools record_sale, receive_payment (cash in), adjust_inventory and receive_purchase_order (stock in when the order arrives), with refunds requiring human approval like payments do. Ideally also a Sales & Fulfillment agent that owns them.

B. Real email: tickets coming in and replies going out

Ticket 102 began as "Email: shop rent due in 2 days", which someone had to type into the database. Six drafts (#1–#6) are still sitting on the board. The team can't read the inbox, create a ticket, or send anything. That's on purpose for this homework, but a real shop needs it.

Would need: an email connector exposed as MCP tools: read_inbox → create_ticket for intake, and send_draft that only works after a person approves each message (the same pattern as the approval key). Also an Intake/Communications agent to sort mail onto the right ticket and keep one current draft per requester, not three.

C. Planning ahead: the October rent problem

The Cash tab ends at $152.00, but lease 1 now shows $2,400 due 2026-10-02. Revenue isn't modeled, desk.date_today never advances, and no tool can forecast cash, change lease terms or borrow. If someone files the October rent today, request_payment_approval refuses it ($2,400 > $152), and that's all the team can do. Accounting even noticed this on #103, but nothing could act on it.

Would need: an advance_date / scheduling tool; a forecast_cash tool comparing upcoming obligations with expected sales; a lease-update tool for a payment plan with the landlord (human-approved); and a connection to the bank or a line of credit. Also a Finance/Treasury agent whose job is to see 10-02 coming weeks ahead, rather than answering one ticket at a time.

The biggest lesson: every ticket "succeeded", yet the business ended the day in worse shape. The shop has $152 against a $2,400 bill in 32 days. A team that only works one ticket at a time can't see that. For Campus Customs, the next investment shouldn't be a sixth ticket-solving agent. It should be forward-looking tools: sales recording and cash forecasting.
